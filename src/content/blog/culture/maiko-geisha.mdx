Kyoto's geisha districts are working neighborhoods, not attractions. The maiko and geiko you see are on their way to appointments, not on display.

## The Short Version

- **Maiko are apprentices. Geiko are fully qualified geisha.** Look at the obi: a maiko's trails almost to the ground.
- **Your best chance of seeing one is a weekday evening, 5–7pm, on Hanamikoji in Gion.** It is never guaranteed.
- **Never block, follow, call out to or touch them**, and do not photograph them without clear consent.
- **Stay out of the private alleys.** They are closed to visitors and signposted.
- **To watch them perform, buy a ticket to a seasonal dance.** It costs ¥4,000–¥8,000. A private banquet starts around ¥30,000.

## Maiko or Geiko: How to Tell

| | Maiko | Geiko |
|---|---|---|
| Obi (sash) | Long *darari* obi trailing almost to the ground, marked with her house's crest | Shorter, conventional tie |
| Kimono | Elaborate and theatrical | Subdued, usually darker colors |
| Hair | Seasonal flowers and combs (*kanzashi*) | Smaller, simpler ornaments |
| Sandals | Tall *okobo* platforms, 10–15 cm | Lower sandals |
| Makeup | Heavy white with red lips and red at the eyes | Less theatrical |

The obi is the easiest tell, then the height of the sandals.

## Where and When to See Them

- **Where:** Hanamikoji Street south of Shijo and the Shimbashi area, both in [Gion](/blog/neighborhoods/gion). Miyagawacho and Ponto-cho are quieter second choices.
- **When:** weekday evenings, about 5pm–7pm, when they walk from their house (*okiya*) to the teahouse. Tuesday to Thursday has the fewest tourists. Be on Hanamikoji by 5:30pm.
- **Odds:** on a quiet weekday evening you will more likely than not see at least one. Crowds do not help, because they keep their schedule either way.

## How to Behave

1. **Step aside.** They are professionals walking to work. Do not block the path.
2. **Do not call out, follow or ask for a pose.** They will not stop for photos.
3. **Do not photograph them without clear consent**, and never push a camera in their face.
4. **Do not touch them or their kimono.** It has happened often enough to need saying.

<TipBox type="warning" title="PRIVATE ALLEYS AND PHOTO SIGNS">
Private side alleys off Hanamikoji are closed to visitors, and the signs warn of fines. Other signs in Gion ban photography. They exist because harassment by tourists became a real problem. Stay on the main street and follow every sign you see.
</TipBox>

## Ways to See Them Perform

- **Seasonal dances (*odori*):** the easiest and most respectful way. Miyako Odori (Gion Kobu, April), Kyo Odori (Miyagawacho, April), Kamogawa Odori (Ponto-cho, May), Kitano Odori (Kamishichiken, March–April) and Gion Odori (Gion Higashi, early November). Tickets are ¥4,000–¥8,000, the dearer ones with a bowl of tea before the show, and they sell out, so book ahead through official channels.
- **Private banquets (*ozashiki*):** normally by introduction only. A few ryokan and hotels arrange them for visitors, from around ¥30,000 per person up to ¥80,000 or more. The Gion Hatanaka ryokan also runs a shared dinner evening with maiko for about ¥20,000. Be wary of cheap "geisha dinner" packages.
- **Festivals:** maiko sometimes appear at public events during the [Gion Matsuri](/blog/seasonal/gion-matsuri) and other major festivals.

## The Five Districts

- **Gion Kobu:** the largest and most prestigious, centered on Hanamikoji.
- **Gion Higashi:** smaller and quieter, next to Gion Kobu.
- **Miyagawacho:** between Gion and the Kamo River.
- **Ponto-cho:** the narrow alley along the Kamo River between Shijo and Sanjo.
- **Kamishichiken:** the oldest, near Kitano Tenmangu Shrine in northern Kyoto.
