faq:
  - q: "What is Kyoto cuisine called?"
    a: "Kyoto's traditional cuisine is called *kyo-ryori*. It encompasses several formal styles — kaiseki (multi-course haute cuisine), obanzai (Kyoto home cooking), shojin ryori (Buddhist vegetarian cuisine), and tofu dishes. Each reflects the city's landlocked geography and temple culture."
  - q: "Is Kyoto food expensive?"
    a: "It ranges widely. A bowl of ramen or obanzai lunch costs ¥800–¥1,500. Casual kaiseki (set lunch) runs ¥2,500–¥5,000. Full traditional kaiseki dinners at top restaurants start at ¥15,000 and can exceed ¥50,000."
  - q: "What is tofu so special in Kyoto?"
    a: "Kyoto has exceptionally soft water (one of Japan's softest), which produces tofu with an unusually delicate, silky texture. Kyoto-style tofu (*kyo-dofu*) is lighter and more refined than the firmer tofu used in other regions."
  - q: "Where is the best place to eat cheap and well in Kyoto?"
    a: "Nishiki Market for snacks and street food. The Teramachi-Sanjo area for ramen, donburi, and izakayas. Department store basements (depachika) for bento and prepared foods at reasonable prices."

Kyoto has no coastline, so it never built a fresh-sushi tradition. Its court and temples produced tofu, pickles, vegetables and kaiseki instead.

## The Short Version

- **The one splurge: a kaiseki lunch set.** ¥3,500–¥7,000 for the same seasonal format as dinner, and easier to book.
- **The everyday meal: obanzai or ramen.** Both come in under ¥2,500.
- **Snack at Nishiki Market** for ¥200–¥600 a piece. Go before 10:30am on a weekday.
- **Taste what Kyoto does best:** soft tofu, pickles, matcha and Fushimi sake.
- **Reserve kaiseki days or weeks ahead.**

## The 15 Things to Try

<ImageCaption src="/images/blog/inline/kyoto-food-kaiseki-course.jpg" alt="Multi-course kaiseki meal in a traditional Kyoto restaurant with lacquerware and seasonal ingredients" badge="KAISEKI" caption="A kaiseki set lunch: seasonal ingredients, one small course at a time." />

| Dish | What it is | Where and how much |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Kaiseki** | Kyoto's multi-course haute cuisine. Each dish uses an ingredient at its seasonal peak | Traditional restaurants. Dinner ¥8,000–¥50,000+ |
| **2. Obanzai** | Kyoto home cooking: small dishes of simmered vegetables, pickles, tofu and sometimes small fish | Nishiki and Teramachi areas. Lunch sets ¥1,200–¥2,500 |
| **3. Kyo-tofu** | Silk-soft tofu made with Kyoto's soft water. Try *yudofu* (simmered) or *hiyayakko* (cold) | Tofu restaurants such as Tousuiro |
| **4. Yatsuhashi** | The classic souvenir: soft cinnamon rice dough folded into triangles around red bean paste. Also sold as a hard baked biscuit | Everywhere. The best shops make it fresh |
| **5. Matcha** | Soft serve, parfait and roll cake, made with tea from nearby Uji. Best of all is a whisked bowl | Nishiki Market and Ninenzaka for sweets. See the [Tea Ceremony Guide](/blog/culture/tea-ceremony) |
| **6. Kaiseki lunch set** | 5–8 courses in the full seasonal format, at a fraction of the dinner price | Kaiseki restaurants, by reservation. ¥3,500–¥7,000 |
| **7. Kyoto-style udon** | Noodles in a light, almost clear dashi broth. In winter, *nabeyaki udon* served in an earthenware pot | Udon shops in the Nishiki and Teramachi arcades |
| **8. Fu** | Wheat gluten from temple cooking, chewy and silky. Grilled with miso (*fu no dengaku*) or added to soups | Obanzai and temple-cuisine restaurants |
| **9. Kyoto ramen** | A rich chicken white broth (*tori paitan*) or chicken-soy broth, topped with green onions | Kawaramachi and Shijo areas. ¥850–¥1,200 |
| **10. Tsukemono** | Kyoto pickles: *shibazuke* (purple eggplant and shiso), *suguki* (fermented turnip), *senmaizuke* (thin-sliced turnip) | Nishiki Market, where you can taste before buying |
| **11. Kamo-nasu** | A large, round Kyoto eggplant with dense, creamy flesh. Grilled with miso | Summer menus |
| **12. Saba-zushi** | Pressed sushi of salted mackerel on vinegared rice. Made a day ahead and served at room temperature | Izuju on Shijo |
| **13. Warabi-mochi** | Soft, translucent bracken-starch dumplings with roasted soybean flour and black sugar syrup | A proper wagashi shop, not a convenience store |
| **14. Fushimi sake** | Clean, slightly sweet sake from the soft-water brewery district in south Kyoto | Fushimi breweries such as the Gekkeikan Okura Sake Museum. Tasting sets ¥400–¥800. See [Sake in Kyoto](/blog/food/sake-kyoto) |
| **15. Kyoto beef** | Wagyu from Kyoto Prefecture. Less famous than Kobe or Matsusaka and easier on the budget | High-end teppanyaki and shabu-shabu restaurants |

<TipBox type="warning" title="BOOK AHEAD FOR KAISEKI">
Most traditional kaiseki restaurants need a reservation days or weeks in advance, lunch included. Without one, try a counter-style restaurant at lunch or ask your hotel concierge to call for you.
</TipBox>

## Where to Eat Cheap and Well

- **Nishiki Market:** snacks and pickles. See the [Nishiki Market Guide](/blog/food/nishiki-market).
- **Teramachi-Sanjo:** ramen, udon and obanzai lunches. Lines at lunch move quickly.
- **Fushimi:** the breweries are a few stops south of Fushimi Inari on the Keihan Line. Do the shrine in the morning and a tasting in the afternoon.
- **Not eating meat or fish?** See [Vegetarian & Vegan Kyoto](/blog/food/kyoto-vegetarian).
